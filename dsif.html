Reconfigurable DSIF-Liner Injection Molding System

D&D

Project Summary

In this team project, we designed and prototyped a reconfigurable mold system for DSIF-liner injection molding. The goal was to support, cool, and eject liners and part geometries of varying shapes while adhering to industry standards for injection molding.

Design Goals

  • Build a modular mold system compatible with DSIF-liners.
  • Ensure proper cooling and ejection for parts of various geometries.
  • Maintain manufacturing robustness and industrial compatibility.

Needs, Requirements, and Specifications

We based our needs off of client interviews, background research, and member experience with IM. We simultaneously identified primary, secondary, and tertiary stakeholders.

Full Product Architecture and Functional Diagrams

Based on the needs and requirements tables, we then created a functional tree and flow diagram. It includes inputting power for devices such as cooling and ejection. It also accepts molten plastic, supports the liner, cools, and ejects the parts.
Functional Tree

Next we made a flow diagram that illustrates two primary processes: mold setup and injection molding the part. The mold setup takes human energy as well as the liners and mold to produce the full mold system. The mold system is then input into the process of cooling and ejecting the parts. This process also requires a power input and molten plastic material input, as well as a signal transmission throughout. The final output of the process is the injection molded part. Other steps involved in injection molding the parts that are related to the IM machine were excluded as they don’t directly pertain to our mission statement, so our solution does not intend to modify them.

Functional Flow Diagram
Based on our needs and metrics, and functional tree and flow diagrams, it was apparent that we had three different crytical systems from which to solve: backing system, ejecting system, and cooling system.

Backing System

The figure below shows the concept of our backing system, which was defined as our most critical system and the first one we developed. We first researched existing solutions to gain inspiration as well as an understanding of what we needed to achieve in order to develop the backing system. This also gave us insight into the benefits and drawbacks of existing solutions. The backing is essential to be able to support various liner geometries and produce parts within acceptable tolerances, which is reflected in our needs and requirements above. As shown in the CAD model, the design space for this system was pre-defined by our client's mold geometry. We eventually chose to use casted pewter as our backing system.

The original ejection system used at Ford Prototyping Lab pushed the part out from the bottom half of the mold. However, in our case, the part was sticking to the top half, requiring a modification to the design. To address this, we added an undercut to the ejection pin ensuring the part would stay attached to the bottom and could then be ejected by pushing it downward slightly with the lever. We also decided to place the ejection pin at the start of the runner, where the sprue is located, this way we minimized defects on the part. This setup is illustrated below:

Ejection System with undercut
Drawings by Gabriela Oyarzun B.
The ejection system worked consistently throughout our full prototype testing, reliably ejecting parts in 2–3 seconds. We iterated on the notch and pin length to balance retention and release, ensuring the part would stick just enough to the pin during molding but come off easily during ejection. We ultimately removed the notch and set the pin to protrude about ½", so that when lowered, it sat flush with the liner surface. This allowed the part to be swept off the mold with minimal effort.

Cooling System

Our needs and requirements also reflect the need to properly cool the mold, so we would need to implement an external cooling system throughout the mold. Due to the geometry of the mold and the size of the cavity, the space we had to work with for cooling was also limited. As explained in the Cooling System section, we ended up with one aluminum cooling pipe through each mold half.

Conclusion

This project successfully demonstrated the design, development, and evaluation of a modular injection molding system tailored for interchangeable liner geometries. Through a careful balance of performance, manufacturability, and cost, my team and I developed a mold system that meets tight geometric tolerances, integrates efficient cooling and ejection mechanisms, and is safe and easy to reconfigure. By defining clear metrics and aligning them with user needs, we ensured that each subsystem contributed to the overall success of the design. The final prototype met the majority of performance goals and validated the feasibility of a low-cost, rapidly adaptable mold architecture suitable for research and educational environments.
