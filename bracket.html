CNC Mill Bracket

D&D

Project Summary

As part of ME 240: Introduction to Mechanical Design and Manufacturing, I was tasked with designing a custom mounting bracket for a SainSmart Genmitsu 3020-Pro Max CNC milling machine. The bracket had to accommodate a sensor while ensuring mechanical performance under strict criteria.

Design Goals

  • Redesign the spindle holder to accommodate a new sensor without compromising machine functionality.
  • Maintain cutting performance by minimizing deflection under load.
  • Reduce bracket weight as much as possible from original design (400 gr).
  • Ensure ease of assembly and disassembly of the bracket.

Performance Criteria

  • Resist cutting forces of 100 N in the X, Y, and Z directions.
  • Limit deflection at the spindle tip to ±2 mm in each direction (X, Y, Z).
  • Material: Nylon 12 (E = 1850 MPa, G = 660 MPa) using SLS 3D printing.

Mounting & Geometry Constraints

  • Mount to an M5 bolt grid with proper clearance and tool access.
  • Clamp a 52 mm diameter spindle, centered 100 mm from the mounting face.
  • Fit within a bounding box: 80 mm (X) × 132.5 mm (Y) × 52 mm (Z).
  • Avoid the sensor pocket region.
  • Use simple cross sections for easier beam bending and FEA validation.
After all my group and I had this criteria and constraints jolted down, we started designing our first version.

Design Process

Version 1

  • Created an initial bracket geometry based on hand-calculated forces and stress distributions.
  • Ran FEA simulations in all three axes (X, Y, Z) to validate stress and displacement ranges. Noted high stress concentrations at the base/neck intersection ➝ added fillets and extra material at the spindle mount.
  • Sent in for manufacturing and tested it. The process simulated real-world machining forces and included three distinct directional load tests: X, Y, and Z.
    • Setup: The bracket was mounted to a CNC Z-axis holder using M5 bolts, ensuring full contact with flat mounting surfaces to avoid stress concentrations or fractures.
    • Load Testing: Forces (~100 N) were applied separately in X, Y, and Z directions using calibrated load cells. Plunge indicators were used to measure deflection at two reference points before and after force application.
    • Measurement Protocol: Measurements were taken without moving the indicators between readings to maintain accuracy. TAs assisted in verifying displacement using standard mechanical gauges.

Test Results

Test Metric Result Comments
Max Deflection (X) ~1.2 mm Within spec (≤ 2 mm)
Max Deflection (Z) ~1.6 mm Matches hand-calculated prediction
Max Stress (Y-axis) ~85 MPa Concentrated at neck-to-base transition
Safety Factor ~2.1 Acceptable for non-critical mounting part
Weight 204.2 g Reduced in Version 2

Version 2

  • Shifted structural support from neutral axis (mid-body) to top and bottom edges.
  • Added top struts and removed excess material using cutouts and curves to reduce weight.
  • Weight: 193g
    • Tradeoff: Slight increase in X-deflection, but still within spec (≤ 2mm)

Test Results

Test Metric Result Comments
Max Deflection (X) ~1.8 mm Increased slightly, still within spec (≤ 2 mm)
Max Deflection (Z) ~1.6 mm No change from Version 1
Max Stress (Y-axis) ~83 MPa Stress more evenly distributed due to struts
Material Aluminum 6061-T6 Same as Version 1
Safety Factor ~2.2 Slight improvement from added supports
Weight 193 g Optimized with cutouts and curves

Key Takeaways / Conclusions:

Through iterative design, simulation, and hands-on testing, we successfully developed and validated a CNC spindle bracket capable of withstanding directional machining loads. Our first version revealed critical stress concentrations, which we addressed in Version 2 through geometry refinement and material redistribution. Final testing in X, Y, and Z axes confirmed the design’s structural integrity, with measured displacements closely aligning with FEA predictions. And what we were most proud of: weight reduction - we reduced the weight of the bracket to 48.25% of its original design weight. This project reinforced our understanding of structural mechanics, precision mounting, and the practical nuances of translating digital designs into functional, manufacturable components.
