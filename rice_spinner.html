Rice Spinner: A Sustainable Kitchen Appliance

As part of my Design Thinking and Communication (DTC) course at Northwestern, my team and I developed a low-cost, water-saving rice washing device for home use. This project began with extensive ethnographic research into rice preparation rituals and sustainability habits, leading to a user-driven product that saves time, water, and mess in the kitchen.

Project Goal

Create a sustainable, efficient tool for rinsing rice that reduces water usage and improves the user experience for everyday home cooks.

Design Process

Research:

To start our design process, we conducted over 20 interviews with users ranging from college students to professional chefs. Additionally we conducted user observations, where we found key pain points:
  • Difficulty washing rice without spilling grains
  • No standard method for determining when rice is clean
  • Excessive water usage (over 2 gallons per wash on average)
  • Frustration with transferring wet rice between containers
These painpoints allowed us to develop a list of needs and requierements we kept in mind during the brainstorming and prototyping phase, from which we also developed two primary personas:
  • "The Efficient Home Cook" – values speed and convenience
  • "The Mindful Chef" – prioritizes sustainability and precision

These findings directly shaped our problem statement and initial product requirements: a low-effort, water-efficient tool for rinsing rice that retains cultural familiarity while improving usability.

Brainstorming:

To kick off the design process, we began with a brainstorming session to explore a wide range of ideas. Our goal was to identify potential solutions that addressed user pain points, functional requirements, and constraints such as cost, manufacturability, and ease of use. After the session was done, we were able to cluster our ideas into four categories: hand tools, spinners, shakers, and filters.

Iterative Prototyping:

Key insights from user observations and interviews included the importance of wall height to prevent splashing, basket containment to minimize rice loss, and mechanical leverage for spinning efficiency. Because of this, we chose to adapt a conventional salad spinner because it offered a simple, intuitive, and proven method for agitating and spinning, which is exactly what we needed to clean rice effectively. Additionally, the salad spinner’s mechanism allowed us to rapidly prototype using familiar parts while focusing our design efforts on rice-specific constraints such as smaller grain size and finer filtration needs. This approach gave us a practical foundation to build on while saving time and reducing complexity during early development.

We decided to add flaps to the spinner to have more friction applied towards the rice grains, and tested three different flaps: (1) silicone flaps, (2), polypropylene flaps without holes and contours to bowl shape, and (3) polypropylene flaps with holes. Our objective was to determine which flap cleaned the rice the most effectively (measured through how much water was used) and comfortably.

We conducted real rice rinsing tests using these flap prototypes under running water to simulate actual use. Through this testing, we identified common pain points such as:

  • Water not draining quickly enough due to tight mesh geometry
  • Rice grains escaping through wide gaps in early filter concepts
  • Difficulty maintaining balance while spinning by hand

These observations directly shaped our design refinements, including the placement of internal supports, improvements to drainage pathways, and exploration of different rotational interfaces.

Conclusions and Impact:

From the data obtained from the testing, it can be concluded that the silicone flap is the easiest to crank compared to the polypropylene flaps, which are stiff and meet more resistance. Although the polypropylene with holes flap agitates well, the drawback of the crank needing more force makes the silicone the best choice.
