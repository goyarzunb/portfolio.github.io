Toy Design and Manufacturing

Injection Molded Part and Cavity Mold Design and Manufacturing

Project Summary

In ME 340-2: Computer-Integrated Manufacturing CAD/CAM, my team designed and manufactured an injection-molded fish toy with multiple assembly features and geometry constraints. I was responsible for the CAD and CAM design and manufacturing of the right-side cavity mold, which required close consideration of parting surfaces, draft angles, runner and gate layout, cooling, and manufacturability.

Part Design Overview

Design Parameter Description
Wall thickness Recommended: 0.030 in ≤ wall thickness ≤ 0.125 in (0.762 mm ≤ wall thickness ≤ 3.175 mm)
Our Design: 0.040 inch (1.016mm) uniform wall thickness.
Runner Design Recommended: The minimum distance between the end if the runner and the wall must be 0.030 inches (0.762 mm).
Our Design: Runner to wall distance 0.0670 in (1.7018 mm)
Gate Position Recommended: The gate should be placed to minimize weld lines, reduce pressure drop, and allow even filling.
Our Design: Both the runner and the gate were placed at the top of the fish to ensure even filling, minimizing weld lines.
Draft angle Recommended: For PE: 1.5 – 3.0 degrees
Our Design: Fish's natural curvature eliminates the need for a draft in most areas. The pectoral fin had a 2-degree draft angle on the back side
Parting line Recommended: The projected area of the parting line should be the maximum profile of the part. (Widest point)
Our Design: Through fish centerline
Material Polyethylene (PE)



Additionally, our design has no sharp corners and all edges and corners have edge blends and fillets. This way we can create less stress concentrations - which usually lead to part failure or cracking, and have better material flow when injecting the molten plastic into the molds.

Assembly Methods

Our design consists of two parts: the left and right sides of the fish, with a parting line along the vertical central axis when viewed from the front. To assemble them, we opted for an interference fit for the pins and holes, primarily because it is simpler and easier to manufacture than snap fits. It also provides a secure, semi‐permanent assembly that ensures a tight, reliable joint. Snap fits can degrade over time, especially with repeated assembly, and can introduce overhangs hindering proper mold ejection. Because we sought to avoid such issues, we selected the interference fit method.

Interference Fit for Injection Molded Assembly

To ensure a secure interference fit between the fish body halves, we selected a dowel pin to act as the insert shaft. Based on guidelines recommending a pin diameter around 3× the wall thickness, we used a 0.125 in steel-hardened dowel pin. We wanted the wall thickness around the hole to match our uniform wall thickness of ~0.040 in, but we added 0.0056 in to account for shrinkage during injection molding, giving us a final hole wall thickness of 0.0456 in. This led to an outer hole diameter of 0.1706 in. Using an online calculator and the interference equations from lecture, we solved for the ideal pin diameter:
  • Hole inner diameter (d₁): 0.125 in
  • Hole outer diameter (D): 0.1706 in
  • Calculated pin diameter (d): 0.127 in
  • Resulting interference (I = d − d₁): 0.002 in

CAM and Machining

Right Cavity Mold CAM Operations
By Gabriela Oyarzun B.

Operation Justification
Roughing 1 (Cavity Mill) 0.5 in EM roughing cavity mill to cut off most material out of the blank to create the cavity.
Roughing 2 (Cavity Mill) 0.5 in BM roughing cavity mill to cut off sharp edges left by the previous operation.
Finishing 1 (Cavity Mill) 0.5 in BM finishing cavity mill to refine the part's dimensions and surface quality before tool size reduction (best practice)
Roughing 3 (Area Mill) 0.375 in BM roughing area mill to maintain a consistent chip load and material removal rate across the area for less tool wear.
Finishing 2 (Area Mill) 0.375 in BM finishing area mill before tool size reduction.
Roughing 4 (Cavity Mill) First operation on runner to take most material out.
Roughing 5 (Area Mill) 0.25 in BM roughing on the body of the fish to take off excess material left by previous operations and to start making the cavity on the fin.
Finishing 3 (Area Mill) 0.25 in BM finishing on both body and runner before tool size reduction.
Roughing 6 (Area Mill) 0.125 in BM roughing operation to take off more material from the runner and pectoral fin.
Finishing 4 (Area Mill) 0.125 in BM finishing operation on runner, side and pectoral fins, to give them a smoother surface finish before sizing down a tool.
Roughing 7 (Area Mill) 0.0625 in BM roughing operation on the whole body of the fish and runner to take off any bit of excess material that could’ve been left on previous operations.
Finishing 5 (Floor Wall) 0.0625 in EM finishing operation to remove the rest of material from the dorsal fins.
This CAM setup worked out great. Surface finish did not deviate a lot from the original design. The part that had the roughest surface finish was the tail, with around 11 thousands of an inch of difference from the original design.

Key Takeaways / Conclusions:

In this project, we successfully designed, manufactured and molded a two-part fish assembly using injection molding. During design, we considered wall thickness, draft angles, sharp corners, and interference fits for assembly that resulted in a robust product. During the mold manufacturing process we encountered a few problems with the surface finish and the steel hardened pins that took some time to fix. Furthermore, during the injection molding process, we also had some issues that took us some time to understand and fix, such as mold misalignment, gate thickness, and balancing mold parameters. Once we found the optimal combination of injection pressure, injection time, and melt temperature, we were able to deliver parts within the acceptable tolerances.

Overall, this was a great way to apply the concepts learned in class in a hands-on environment. Some improvements if we were to do this again would be to implement a better method to manufacture the mating holes for the pins. Using a slightly bigger diameter for the outer hole would have allowed us to fit a tool between the holes, and in turn avoid using the steel hardened pins. This project highlighted the importance of optimizing the manufacturing process and proved that even a simple part, like our fish, is very complex and time consuming to produce.
